Background: 
Katrina hurracine (2005) was one of the costliest storm that ever hit the Gulf Coast. New Orleans residents were specifically impacted by Katrina, 455,000 people were forced to evacuate, resulting the population falling from 484,600 prior to Katrina to 223,000 residents- a decline of 54% - by July 2006. While some of the Katrina survivors eventually returned to their pre-Katrina neighborhoods, many decided to relocate to places outside of New Orleans. The project examined the return trajectory as well as the factors that influence the migration years later. The analysis here begin to provide insights into mechanisms that contribute to the likelihood of delayed migration return and reinforcing policy guidance for disaster related events in the future. 

Method: 
The Displaced New Orleans Residents Survey (DNORS) collected longitudinal data from individuals who resided in New Orleans prior to Hurracine Katrina, between 2006 and 2009. My survival analysis utilized adult respondent data from the DNORS public use dataset (N = 1761). After applying the adult repondent weights (N = 1200), as well as excluding individual who never left New Orleans (N = 22), the final analytical sample size is 1178. The pre-existing physical/mental health condition variables have included a list of 7 physical health conditions(high blood pressure, high blood sugar, chronic bronchitis, coronary heart disease, arthiritis, asthma, other health problem) and 2 mental health conditions (major depression, emotional/nervous problem). To determine whether a condition was pre-existing at the time of Katrine (2005), the participants' age at diagnosis and age at Katrina are recorded. If a participant's age at diagnosis was less than or equal to their age at Katrina, the condition was classified as pre-existing. Otherwise, it is a post-katrina diagnosis. For each pre-existing condition, I assigned a severity score of 1 (present) or 0 (absent). Physical severity score were calculated by summing scores across seven physical conditions. These are categorized into three levels: 0 = None, 1-2 = Mild, 3-7 = Severe. Mental health severity score were categorized as 0 = No conditions, and 1-2 (presence of at least one mental health condition).

Method part II:
The addition of pre-existing health condition were formatted as numeric/categorical (factor) as needed. To address the missing data, we used multiple imputation rather than complete case analysis. This approach allowed researchers to retain full analytic sample of 1178, which reduced biases under missing data assumptions. The Cox proportional hazards regression models time to return to New Orleans after hurracine Katrina as outcome. The predictor variables (total of 37) included sociodemographics, disaster related factors, newly constructed pre-existing health conditions. Weighted regression was used to account for the complex survey design/improve population level inference by addressing heteroscedasticity. 

Results: 
Five variables: self rated health (SRH), home damage, homeownership status, race, severe pre-existing health conditions emerged as statistically significant predictor of time to return. Specifically, the people with very good/excellent self rated health have higher odds of returning to New Orleans. Additionally, individual with three or more physical conditions prior to Katrina were more likely to return, potentially reflecting dissatisfaction with the quality of care in temporary shelters/replacement housing. Further analysis indicated that hazard ratio for SRH remained relatively stable even after adjusting for pre-existing conditions, suggesting these conditions do not substantially confound the relationship between self rated health and return timing. Lastly, the use of multiple imputation to address missing data, as opposed to complete case analysis, increased our effective sample size by 177 participants. This methdological choice enhanced the precision of our estimates. A larger dataset provided more reliable insights into the population level dynamics of return migration in the aftermath of Hurracine katrina.
